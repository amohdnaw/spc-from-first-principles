Level by level

Level 1

Every process varies, and a histogram shows how much but throws away the order the parts were made in, so a stable process and a drifting one can look identical. You have to tell everyday noise (common cause) from a real event (special cause), because chasing noise by adjusting after every part makes the spread worse.

rightQ1 Yes. One can be a stable process and the other a slow drift that needs an engineer now. Only the time order tells them apart, for example the longest run on one side of the mean (7 versus 121 in the example).
rightQ2 The variation gets worse: the variance doubles and the spread is about 1.4 times wider. Each correction subtracts the last part's random error from this part's, so every result becomes the difference of two random errors instead of one.
  • term 1.5 “$\operatorname{Var}(e_i - e_{i-1}) = 2\sigma^2$” — The symbol sigma (and sigma squared) appears here for the first time with no definition; the glossary gives variance in words but never says what sigma is.
  • prior-knowledge 1.5 “every outcome after the first is a difference of two independent draws. The variance is exactly doubled” — Why subtracting one random error from another doubles the variance (rather than cancelling) is asserted, not shown; I only accept it because I know variances of independent quantities add even under subtraction.
  • prior-knowledge 1.5 “variance = ×2 exactly; spread = ×1.414” — Going from doubled variance to spread times root 2 needs the fact that spread is the square root of variance, which the page has not stated.
  • jump 1.5 interactive “multiplies the variance by exactly 2 / (2 − g)” — A new formula for partial correction appears with no derivation and no way to see where it comes from.
  • term 1.5 interactive “The amber lines are ±3σ of the machine left alone” — Why plus or minus three sigma is a meaningful band is not explained; a newcomer does not know what the lines are for.

Level 2

A probability is what a repeated process does in the long run, never a promise about the next part; the coin has no memory, so nothing is 'owed' and the proportion settles only because the count outgrows the gap. A chart's 0.27 % is the rate it false-alarms on an unchanged process, which is not a guarantee of one alarm in 370.

rightQ3 One half. The coin has no memory, so earlier heads change nothing; the site showed the share of heads after six heads was still about 0.50.
rightQ4 No. The chance of at least one false alarm over 370 subgroups is about 63 %, not 100 %, and half of first alarms come by about subgroup 256. I could quote these numbers but could not have computed them myself.
Right, but the reader quoted 63 % without being able to compute it: the complement rule is never taught.
  • other 2.1 “Level 1 ended with a shape nobody chose.” — Level 1 ended on Deming's funnel, not on a 'shape', so the back-reference does not land and the reader wonders what they missed.
  • jump 2.2 “$\mathbb{E}|S_n| = 2^{1-n} n \binom{n-1}{\lfloor (n-1)/2 \rfloor}$ , which for any n worth plotting is $\sqrt{2n/\pi}$” — An exact formula with binomial coefficients and its square-root approximation appear with no derivation; the E-bar notation is also used before expectation is defined in 2.4.
  • jump 2.2 “Its error is that same quantity divided by the number of flips” — Gap 8 divided by 100 flips gives 0.08, but the margin says 0.03979; the figure shows it is divided by 2n, so a reader checking the arithmetic is stuck.
  • jump 2.5 “Run the 370 that the phrase “one alarm in 370” actually names” — The number 370 has never been introduced; a newcomer must work out alone that it is 1 divided by 0.0027.
  • prior-knowledge 2.5 “$1-(1-\alpha)^{1/\alpha} \;\longrightarrow\; 1-e^{-1} \approx 0.632$” — The 'one minus chance of no alarm in n tries' rule, what alpha is, and why the limit is 1 minus 1/e are all unexplained; I only follow it because I know the complement rule and the limit definition of e.
  • prior-knowledge 2.5 “because the waiting time is geometric, the typical wait is shorter than the average one” — 'Geometric' and 'median' are not defined, and why a skewed wait puts the median below the mean is assumed knowledge.

Carry-over: Level 1 should have ended on the 'shape nobody chose' that Level 2 opens with, or Level 2 should refer back to what Level 1 actually ended on.

Level 3

A pile of readings is summed up by three things: the mean (the balance point where deviations cancel), sigma (square the deviations, average, take the root), and a bell shape that appears on its own when many small effects add up. Any handful of parts only estimates the process's true values, which is why a sample's spread divides by n minus 1.

rightQ5 Because the deviations from the mean cancel to zero by definition, so their plain average is always zero. Squaring makes them all positive; you average and then take the square root to get back to the original units.
rightQ6 Because a sample sits a bit closer to its own mean than to the true process centre, so dividing by n comes out too small. Dividing by n minus 1 makes the average estimate land on the true value; I know the size of the bias is (n-1)/n but not why it is exactly that.
  • jump 3.4 “Many small independent effects, added together, land on this curve.” — The text gives no picture of 'this curve' (only the skipped video shows it) and asserts, without any reason, that adding small effects produces a bell.
  • jump 3.5 “Twelve parts spread around their own mean sit a little tighter than they do around the truth” — The reason is given loosely, but why the fix is exactly n minus 1 (and not, say, n minus 2) is never shown; the lab only states the (n−1)/n factor.
  • term 3.5 interactive “Average s², ÷ n” — The symbol s (sample spread, as opposed to sigma) is used in the lab without being introduced in the text.

Carry-over: Level 1 already used sigma, variance and the root-2 spread argument; this is the level that defines sigma, so Level 1's maths arrives two chapters before its tools.

Level 4

Single parts are unpredictable, but averages of several parts behave by a law: their spread is one part's spread divided by the square root of how many you averaged, and they form a bell even when the parts do not. The gain flattens fast, so small subgroups of four or five are the sweet spot, and charts plot subgroup means so a shift stands out.

rightQ7 The averages spread half as much, because the spread shrinks by the square root of 4, which is 2.
rightQ8 Because means of a few parts cluster much tighter than single parts, so a real shift in the process moves the mean far enough to see instead of being lost in part-to-part noise.
  • jump 4.1 “Ten rolls tell you nothing: the worst face is out by two hundred percent.” — It is not said what is being compared (a face's count against the expected 1.67 rolls), so 'two hundred percent' cannot be checked.
  • figure 4.2 “Measured spreads land exactly on σ/√k — 2.449 → 0.447” — The figure shows one die's sigma as 1.71 and the 30-dice value as 0.312, so the caption's 2.449 and 0.447 match nothing on the chart; the unlabelled 0 to 1.6 vertical axis is also not a count like earlier histograms.
  • jump 4.3 “Two numbers, measured separately, agree to three decimals” — The root-n law is shown only as a measured coincidence; the reason it must hold (the derivation the caption says the video performs) never appears in the text, and 'standard error' is named without explanation.
  • jump 4.4 “The second part halves your uncertainty” — By the page's own formula two parts give sigma over root 2, about 0.71, a 29 % cut not a half; a reader checking the arithmetic cannot make it work.
  • figure 4.4 “with sigma set to one, then walk the subgroup size from one to twenty-five” — The figure's vertical axis starts at 0.080 mm, not at one sigma, so the text's 'sigma set to one' does not match the chart in front of the reader.

Level 5

Every number on a chart is an estimate from a handful of parts, and its typical error is the standard error, sigma over root n. A '95 %' interval is a procedure that catches the truth 95 times in 100, and when the spread is itself estimated from few parts you must use a wider t value instead of 1.96 or it only catches about 88 %; tighter intervals cost parts by the square.

rightQ9 Because with 5 parts the spread is also estimated and is itself uncertain, so a 1.96 interval is too narrow and only catches the truth about 88 % of the time. The t value (2.776 at five parts) widens it so it really delivers 95 %. I could not say where 2.776 comes from.
Right, but 'I could not say where 2.776 comes from'.
rightQ10 Four times as many, because the width shrinks with the square root of n, so halving it needs n times four (5 parts becomes 20).
  • jump 5.2 “An interval is the estimate plus and minus a margin.” — The size of the margin is never written down for the basic case, so a reader cannot see how an interval is actually built before the text starts counting how often it misses.
  • prior-knowledge 5.2 “With z = 1.96 the promised 95% delivers 87.9%” — Why 1.96 standard errors should give 95 % (and what z is) is never explained; I only follow it because I already know 1.96 is the two-sided 95 % point of the normal curve.
  • jump 5.3 “$\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$” — What t is, why it carries the subscript n−1, and where 2.776 comes from are never shown; the reader must take the number on trust.
  • term 5.5 “Every control chart ahead multiplies a range by a constant called $d_2$” — Neither 'range' nor what d2 converts (range into sigma) is explained, so the reader has no idea what is being simulated or why it matters.
  • jump 5.5 “earning the third decimal from simulation alone would take 11.5 million subgroups” — The 11.5 million figure appears with no working, and 'the exact integral' is mentioned with no hint of what integral.

Carry-over: Level 3 gave the normal (bell) curve no picture or name and no link between sigma and the share of results inside a band, which this level needs for 1.96 and 95 %.

Level 6

If the process has not changed, every subgroup mean comes from one known bell curve, and that 'nothing changed' is the claim the chart tests. Limits at plus and minus three sigma hold 99.73 % of that curve, so a point outside happens by chance only about 1 time in 370, which makes it evidence that something changed, not a verdict on a part.

rightQ11 It does not mean that part is bad; it is evidence against the claim that the process is unchanged, since a stable process puts a point there only about once in 370 subgroups. You should go and find what changed rather than scrap the part.
rightQ12 The area of the bell between minus three and plus three sigma is 99.73 %, leaving 0.27 % in the two tails; one divided by 0.0027 is about 370. I accept the 99.73 % because the page says it is an integral, but I could not compute it.
  • prior-knowledge 6.2 “$\Phi(3) - \Phi(-3) = 0.9973$” — Phi, 'standard normal CDF' and 'erf' are never explained, and the idea that the share inside the limits is the area under the curve needs calculus the reader was not given; I only follow because I know the normal table.
  • term 6.2 interactive “Limit at ± 3.00 σx̄” — The prose says 'three sigma' while the lab and figure say three sigma-x-bar, and the page never says plainly that the limits use the spread of the means, not of single parts.
  • figure 6.4 “The limits are the boundary we just drew, turned on its side.” — No control chart has been shown anywhere in the text, so the reader must imagine rotating a bell into a time chart that only the skipped video draws.
  • jump 6.4 “say 4.1σ above the centre line, where the null allows one point in 370” — One in 370 is the chance of landing beyond 3 sigma, not at 4.1 sigma, so the reader cannot tell what 4.1 adds or how rare it really is.
  • term 6.5 “which is why Phase I needs enough data before the limits mean anything” — 'Phase I' is never defined, and the glossary has no entry for it.
  • prior-knowledge 6.6 “$d_2$ is the expected range of n ⟨standard normals⟩.” — 'Standard normal' (a bell with mean 0 and sigma 1) has no definition, and why dividing the average range by d2 gives sigma is understood only if you already know the idea.
  • jump 6.6 “$A_2$ , $D_3$ and $D_4$ are the numbers printed on every shop-floor chart form.” — The page never says what A2, D3 and D4 do or how limits are actually calculated from them; the figure brings in d3 and an 'R-chart', both unexplained.

Carry-over: Level 5 mentioned d2 and range without defining them, and no earlier level shows what a control chart looks like, yet this level leans on both.

Level 7

A chart can be wrong in two ways: crying wolf when nothing changed, and staying quiet when the process really moved, and the second is far more common because one point rarely sees a small shift. The extra run rules look at patterns across several points; they always cost about four times more false alarms, and whether that is worth it depends on how small a shift you need to catch.

rightQ13 About 2.3 %, so the chart misses it about 97.7 % of the time. I remember the number from the page; I could not have worked it out.
Right, but recalled, not worked out: normal tail areas are never taught.
rightQ14 All four Western Electric rules together cut the time between false alarms from about 377 to 91 subgroups, about four times the false alarms. They are worth it against small shifts like 1 sigma, caught about 4.6 times sooner, but hardly help against a 3 sigma jump, which rule 1 already catches.
  • term 7.1 “A mean that has moved by a full sigma sits almost entirely inside them.” — It is unclear whether 'a sigma' means one part's spread or the spread of the plotted means (the figure says 'sigma of itself'); the two give very different shifts.
  • prior-knowledge 7.1 “So the chance of catching that shift on the next point is 2.3 %.” — The number comes from the bell's tail beyond two sigma, which the page never computes or tabulates; I only see it because I know the normal tail values.
  • prior-knowledge 7.2 “$\text{power}(\delta) = \Phi(-k-\delta) + \Phi(-k+\delta)$” — The formula reuses Phi, which was never explained in words, so a newcomer can read the sentence but not the equation.
  • jump 7.4 “All four together give a false alarm every 91 subgroups instead of 377.” — Levels 2 and 6 said one false alarm in 370 for rule 1, and the page never explains why rule 1 alone now gives 377.
  • jump 7.3 “the answer is a ⟨p-value⟩ of 0.0124 — about one in eighty” — How 0.0124 follows from a point at 2.5 sigma is not shown; the reader must trust it.

Carry-over: Level 6 should have defined Phi and given a way to read tail areas (even a small table), because this level's numbers (2.3 %, 15.9 %, 0.0124) all depend on it.

Level 8

Capability puts the customer's drawing limits and the process's spread on the same axis. Cp is just the tolerance divided by six sigma and ignores where the process is centred; Cpk takes the gap to the nearer limit in units of three sigma, so a drifting mean drags it down, and each Cpk value stands for a defect rate.

rightQ15 Cp is the tolerance width divided by six sigma, a pure width ratio that assumes the process is centred. Cpk uses the distance from the mean to the nearer limit divided by three sigma, so it drops when the mean drifts even if the spread is unchanged.
rightQ16 Because Cpk maps to a defect rate on a steep curve: 1.33 is about 33 parts per million and 1.67 about 0.27, roughly a hundred times less scrap.
  • other 8.1 “Capability compares two distributions. One belongs to the customer and one belongs to the process” — The customer only gives two limit lines, not a pattern of values, so calling it a 'distribution' clashes with the glossary meaning just learned.
  • term 8.2 “$C_{pk} = \dfrac{\min\!\left(\mathrm{USL} - \mu,\ \mu - \mathrm{LSL}\right)}{3\sigma}$” — USL, LSL and mu are not named in the prose (only as figure labels), and the formula for Cpk arrives in the section about Cp, before Cpk is introduced.
  • jump 8.3 “That leak is 8,198 parts per million” — The defect rate comes from a normal tail area the reader has never been shown how to compute, so 8,198 (and 33.04, 0.27) must be taken on trust.
  • figure 8.4 “figure 8.3: The overview act: a chart and its limits drawing themselves from the data” — The image shows a single tail labelled '1 in 741 parts', not a chart drawing its limits, and 741 matches no number in the text.
  • term 8.4 “mapped both short-term and with the 1.5σ drift convention behind “Six Sigma”” — Short-term versus long-term and the 1.5 sigma drift convention appear only in the caption and figure, never explained, and the figure's '0 ppm' at 1.67 contradicts the text's 0.27.
  • other 8.4 “Control limits are what the process says. Spec limits are what the customer says. Cpk is the distance between them.” — This suggests Cpk compares control limits with spec limits, but the formula uses the spread of single parts, while control limits were built from the spread of means; a newcomer will mix the two.

Carry-over: Earlier levels never stated plainly that capability uses the sigma of individual parts while control limits use the sigma of subgroup means.

Level 9

A plain three-sigma chart judges each point alone and forgets it, so a slow drift goes unnoticed until the mean has moved a long way. A chart that keeps a running weighted memory of past points (EWMA), with its limits tuned to give the same false-alarm rate, lets the steady drift add up while noise cancels, and catches it several times sooner.

rightQ17 Because it judges each point on its own and then forgets it, and during a slow drift no single point looks extreme until the mean has moved a lot (2.6 sigma by subgroup 64 in the example).
rightQ18 No. Its limits were set by simulation so it false-alarms exactly as often as the Shewhart chart, about once in 370 quiet subgroups, so the speed-up came free.
  • term 9.1 “Here is a ⟨Shewhart chart⟩ with limits at ±3σ” — 'Shewhart chart' is used as a name for the first time, with no glossary entry and no sentence tying it to the control chart of Level 6.
  • jump 9.2 “That is 43 subgroups after the drift began” — Drift starts at 20 and the alarm comes at 64, which is 44, so a reader checking the figure gets a different number from the text.
  • jump 9.3 “Each new subgroup gets a fifth of the weight and the running statistic keeps the rest” — The update rule for the remembering statistic is never written down, and the name EWMA appears only in an equation caption without being spelled out.
  • jump 9.3 “$\pm L\sigma\sqrt{\dfrac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\left(1-(1-\lambda)^{2i}\right)}$” — L and i are undefined, the square-root factor has no derivation, and the caption's ±3 sigma clashes with the prose saying the limits are not ±3.
  • other 9.5 “Run thousands of them and the average wait to detect a one sigma shift” — The chapter is about a slow drift but the averaged result is for a sudden one-sigma shift (the figure's axis says 'shift when it happens'), so it is unclear which situation the 4.4 times applies to.

Level 10

When you count bad items or flaws instead of measuring sizes, the spread is fixed by the average rate, so there is nothing separate to estimate, and scatter wider than that formula allows means the rate itself moved. You pick one of four count charts by asking whether you count bad items or flaws and whether the subgroup size changes, and you must watch for limits that move with size, a lower limit stuck at zero, and normal-curve tails that understate the risk.

rightQ19 Because for counts the spread is fixed by the average (binomial for bad items, Poisson for flaws), so there is no separate spread to estimate and track. A range chart exists for measurements, where spread is its own fact; I am not fully sure what a range chart shows, as it was never drawn.
Right, but 'I am not fully sure what a range chart shows, as it was never drawn'.
rightQ20 A u-chart: counting defects points to Poisson, and a varying number of items means the size changes, which the selection rule maps to u.
  • prior-knowledge 10.1 “Count defective items and the result is binomial, so its standard deviation is fixed by its mean.” — The formula root of p(1−p)/n is stated, not derived; I accept it only because I already know the binomial variance.
  • prior-knowledge 10.1 “Count defects and the result is Poisson, where the variance is the mean.” — Why a Poisson count has variance equal to its mean is asserted with no reasoning a newcomer could follow.
  • term 10.1 “A measurement needs a range chart beside it.” — A range chart was never introduced or drawn (only an 'R-chart' label in a Level 6 figure), so the central contrast of this section has nothing to stand on.

Level 11

Relating one number to another runs on a single split: total squared scatter equals the part a fitted line explains plus what is left over, and the same split relabelled gives ANOVA and a gauge study. The best line minimises the squared misses; R² is only the explained share, which rises even for noise and can hide a curve, and a forecast for one new part is much wider than one for the average.

rightQ21 No. R² can never fall when a column is added, so pure noise pushes it up while explaining nothing; adjusted R², which penalises extra columns, went down instead.
rightQ22 The interval for a single new reading. It is wider (about four times here) because it must include the part's own random scatter as well as the uncertainty in the line, so more data never shrinks it below the process noise.
  • term 11.1 “$\begin{aligned}\underbrace{\sum (y_i-\bar{y})^2}_{\text{total}} &= \underbrace{\sum (\hat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2}_{\text{explained}}” — y-hat (the value the line predicts) appears before any line or prediction has been introduced, so 'explained' has no meaning yet.
  • prior-knowledge 11.1 “One identity, three names” — That total equals explained plus residual exactly (the cross term vanishing for a least-squares line) is asserted; it is not obvious from algebra and I accept it only from prior knowledge.
  • jump 11.1 “Relabel those two terms between-groups and within-groups and you have ANOVA.” — No groups, example or picture are given, so a newcomer cannot see how the regression terms become between-group and within-group scatter.
  • jump 11.2 “the closed form lands exactly at that minimum rather than near it” — The closed-form slope formula is never shown, so the reader cannot check the claim or compute a line.
  • jump 11.4 “The difference is a single 1 inside a square root” — Neither interval's formula is written, so there is no square root in view to find the 1 in; the margin entry 'at 172 m/min = ;' is also blank.
  • term 11.5 “That is a Gage R&R.” — What a gauge study is for, and why measurement error should be split out at all, is never explained; the four-way split is asserted with no worked step.

Carry-over: No earlier level discusses measurement error as a source of variation (Level 1 mentions gauge resolution only), so the gauge study arrives without motivation.

Level 12

When two factors interact, tuning one factor at a time can stop at the wrong setting even with perfect measurements, because it never tries the combination that wins; a factorial design tries every corner, so it can measure the interaction and estimates main effects more precisely. Fractional designs screen many factors cheaply at the price of tangling effects together, and centre points are needed to spot a curved response the corners cannot see.
