An interactive essay on Statistical Process Control

Four lines are running. One is quietly making scrap.

Below is a simulated plant: four production lines streaming real measurements, right now, in your browser. One of them started drifting a few minutes ago. Most dashboards would show you four green numbers. An SPC engineer sees something different. Scroll — by the end, so will you.

Act I.

The supervisor's view: four numbers, all "fine"

This is what most plants manage on: the latest reading from each line. Target is 50.00 mm, tolerance ±0.30. Every number below is inside tolerance — nothing to see here, right? Watch for a minute. Then decide which line you'd shut down.

Live readings · target 50.000 mm · tol ±0.300 t = 00:00

Line C is the failure — its average has been creeping upward for minutes while every individual reading stayed inside tolerance. That's the trap of managing on last-value: a single number can't show direction. The mean of Line C has moved ~0.15 mm since you started watching. At this rate it breaches USL within the hour — after producing thousands of out-of-tolerance parts. You didn't need more data. You needed memory.

Act II.

The SPC view: same data, but now it has a memory

A control chart doesn't plot parts — it plots evidence over time, against limits learned from the process itself (±3σ of its own history). The drifting line can't hide anymore: watch its points march toward UCL until the chart raises the alarm before a single bad part is made.

X̄ charts · subgroups of 5 · ±3σ limits from baseline alarms: none

Notice when the alarm fires versus when parts actually go out of spec. That gap — often hours of production — is exactly what SPC buys you. It's also why the limits come from the process, not the customer: spec limits say what's acceptable, control limits say what's normal. A process can be perfectly in control and still make garbage — or wildly out of control and passing inspection, like Line C was in Act I.

Act III.

Capability: how much scrap is this process hiding?

Control charts watch stability. Capability asks a harder question: if this process keeps running exactly as it is, how many parts will fail? Drag the process mean and spread. Watch Cpk — and the predicted defect rate — respond.

Cp asks: if the process were perfectly centered, could it fit inside tolerance? Cpk asks the honest question: given where it actually sits, how much room is left on the bad side? A Cpk of 1.33 ≈ 32 defects per million. 1.0 ≈ 1,350. 0.8 ≈ 16,400 per million.

What you just used

Everything on this page runs locally in your browser — no server, no library, no charting package. The plant simulation generates autocorrelated noise with scripted tool-wear drift; the charts, limits, EWMA logic, capability math (normal CDF via erf approximation), and visualizations are hand-built in vanilla JavaScript, Canvas, and SVG.
