Level 12

Experiments

The last level. Change the settings on purpose — and discover that studying one factor at a time can lead you confidently to the wrong answer.

The rest of this curriculum starts by claiming that variation is predictable. That claim already assumes you own three things: what an average actually is, what a standard deviation measures, and where the bell shape comes from. This level supplies them and stops. There are no control charts here.

Twelve shafts, turned to a nominal twelve millimetres, measured to a thousandth. No two are identical. That is not a defect — it is what every real process does, and the whole discipline exists because of it.

The mean is a balance point

Not "the middle", and not "the typical value". The mean is the one place where the deviations cancel: add up how far every part sits above it, add up how far every part sits below it, and the two totals are equal and opposite. That is the definition, and it is why the mean is the natural place to stand when you want to describe a process.

Spread needs squaring

The obvious way to measure spread is to average the deviations. It fails, and it fails for a structural reason: they sum to zero by construction. Every time, for every dataset. The average deviation is not a small number, it is exactly nothing.

So throw the signs away by squaring, average the squares, then take the root to get back into millimetres. That is the standard deviation — not a convention someone chose, but the shortest route around a problem the mean creates.

The fourth term is the whole subject. Set it to zero and one factor at a time works perfectly well, if slowly. Let it be larger than a main effect and the procedure stops at the wrong corner — with no noise required.

The shape is a consequence

Measure twelve parts and you have a scatter. Measure twenty thousand and a shape appears — and it is always the same shape, whenever the variation comes from many small independent causes added together. Nobody imposes the bell. It falls out of the arithmetic, which is exactly why it can be relied on later.

A sample is not the population

These twelve shafts give a mean of 11.9981 and a spread of 0.0133. The process they came from has a true mean of 12.000 and a true sigma of 0.020 — two numbers you will never observe directly. Another twelve parts would hand you slightly different estimates.

That gap between the estimate and the truth is not sloppiness. It is measurable, and quantifying it is precisely what the next level does.
