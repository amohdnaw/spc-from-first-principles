What the numbers mean

The prerequisite. What a mean actually is, why spread has to be squared, and where the bell comes from — measured on twelve real shafts. No control charts.

I
Variation is predictable

Individual parts are unpredictable; aggregates obey a law. One die is chaos, the average of thirty is a bell exactly σ/√30 wide.

σ/√n →
II
Control limits are a hypothesis test

±3σ isn't taste. If the process is stable, a point lands outside 0.27% of the time — so a violation is a bet at 370:1 that something changed.

370:1 →
III
Capability is comparing two distributions

Spec limits are the customer's voice, the spread is the process's. Cpk prices the mismatch as an exact tail integral.

IV
Detection theory: memory beats points

Slow drift never looks alarming in any single point. EWMA accumulates evidence and catches a 1σ shift about four times sooner, at the same false-alarm cost.

The curriculum, applied

Three times that trace was a real line and the fix had to survive an audit. I find the drift before it finds the scrap bin — the two labs below are the actual detection problems, live.

a.

Cutting diameter variation on a CNC line by half

A critical bore was failing Cpk audits (1.1) with no assignable cause. Layering an EWMA chart over the X̄-R exposed slow tool wear invisible to the Shewhart chart. Fixing coolant temperature cycling and tying tool changes to the EWMA signal brought the process back to centre. b.

Proving the measurement system before blaming the process

Two engineers had already tried — and failed — to fix a "chaotic" filling line. A crossed Gage R&R showed the measurement system itself consumed 61% of tolerance. After a fixture redesign, %GRR fell to 9%, and the "process problem" turned out to be over-adjustment. Slide the gage error and watch the verdict flip.

A daily quality dashboard executives actually open
