Why SPC works — derived, not asserted.

Most training teaches control charts as rules to memorize. They're not. Every formula on a shop floor is a consequence of one statistical idea, and each level below builds on the last — ending at a place where you can defend every number in front of an audit. All figures are computed live or rendered from simulations whose source is open.

Level I.

Variation is predictable

One part is noise. Many parts are information.

A single measurement tells you almost nothing — its value is chaos. But averages of repeated measurements obey a law so reliable that entire industries are built on it. Watch one die become a bell as we average more of them; then notice the yellow numbers: the spread shrinks by exactly σ/√n. That single fact is the engine under everything else on this page.

Level II.

Control limits are a hypothesis test

±3σ isn't taste. It's a bet at 370:1 odds.

If a process is stable, Level I's logic tells us exactly which distribution every subgroup mean is drawn from. Choose limits at ±3σ of that distribution and you've defined a test: points inside are boring, a point outside happens 0.27% of the time by pure chance — once per ~370 subgroups. Level III.

Capability is comparing two distributions

Specs are the customer's voice. Spread is the process's. Cpk prices their mismatch in defects.

Control limits describe what a process does; spec limits describe what a customer will accept. Capability indices measure the gap between those two sentences — Cp compares widths, Cpk also accounts for where the mean sits. Level IV.

Detection theory: memory beats points

A chart with a memory catches drift while every raw point still looks innocent.

Slow drift is the most expensive failure mode in manufacturing precisely because no single measurement ever looks alarming. Shewhart charts wait for extremes; EWMA and CUSUM charts accumulate evidence across subgroups. How much sooner?
