1.2

A histogram is an instrument

Pile 240 measurements into bins and a shape appears. same data, two settingsat 4 binsone lumpat 52 binsa combThe shape looks like a fact about the process, and it is not: bin width is a setting on the instrument, and the setting changes the answer.

Four bins says the process is a single lump. Fifty-two says it is a row of spikes. Neither is the process. Somewhere in between is a picture you can read, and knowing that the knob exists is the difference between reading a histogram and believing one.

1.4

Two kinds of variation

Shewhart's distinction, and the one the rest of the subject rests on. the distinctioncommon causethe process being itselfspecial causesomething identifiable happenedSome variation is the process being itself — many small causes, none of them findable, none of them worth chasing. Some variation is something identifiable that happened: a tool changed, a batch differed, an operator was new.

The first kind is called common cause and the second special cause. They demand opposite responses, which is why telling them apart is worth eleven chapters: you improve a common-cause process by changing the process, and a special-cause process by finding the thing that happened.

A histogram cannot tell a stable process from a drifting one. Only the order can.

1.5

Reacting to noise makes it worse

Suppose you cannot tell them apart, and you treat every wobble as a signal: after each part, you correct the machine by exactly what that part was out by. the cost of answering noisevariance×2 exactlyspread×1.414It is the most reasonable-looking policy on a shop floor, and it is Deming's funnel.

Each correction subtracts the previous part's noise from this part's, so every outcome after the first is a difference of two independent draws. The variance is exactly doubled and stays doubled — the spread the customer receives is √2 times wider, bought with a full shift of conscientious work.
